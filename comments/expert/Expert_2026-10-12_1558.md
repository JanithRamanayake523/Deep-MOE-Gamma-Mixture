### Note A: why R0-C-06 (monodromy) does not hold as stated

The critic's argument is that "loops of datasets whose Bayes estimates swap two components" cannot be lifted by a continuous network. Three facts are against this.

1. **The input space is contractible, so lifting is not obstructed.** AEM-Net is a function on multisets of n points in ℝᵏ (or on the positive orthant after canonicalization). That space is the quotient ℝ^{nk}/S_n, which is star-shaped (D ↦ tD), so it is contractible and simply connected.
   - Suppose ϑ̂*(D) is continuous and never has two equal components.
   - Then it maps into the configuration space of distinct unordered points. That space is covered by the space of ordered tuples.
   - The lifting criterion then gives a continuous ordered lift.
   - A loop of data with "swapping" Bayes estimates bounds a disc in data space. The disc's image must reach the collision locus or a discontinuity of ϑ̂*. The obstruction is therefore **not** topological.
2. **The real issue is that ϑ̂* can be discontinuous.** The quotient Fréchet mean can jump when the posterior expected loss has two near-equal minimizers (which matching wins). That is a genuine source of approximation error, and the critic's request to *measure where the error concentrates* is still a good one.
3. **Thm 3(iii) concerns Bayes risk, which needs only L² approximation.**
   - Continuous functions are dense in L²(P_D) for measurable targets, by Lusin's theorem.
   - A measurable ordered selection of ϑ̂* exists by the Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski measurable-selection theorem.
   - The loss is bounded and continuous.

   So inf over the network class of r(φ) equals r(ϑ̂*) for a universal class. The claim "the approximation error cannot be driven to zero" is false in the risk sense.

**What to write in the paper:** one remark. It should say that ϑ̂* may be discontinuous where matchings tie, that discontinuities and collisions are where finite networks will err, and give an empirical map of the error against the Fréchet-mean "margin" (the gap between the best and second-best matching in posterior expected loss). Do **not** state a monodromy theorem. Dym et al. (2024) concerns canonicalization maps defined *on the orbit space itself*. That applies to the alignment step in Prop 2, not to the point estimator.

### Note B: the "pure-L_Q final phase" is solving a non-problem, except for NLL

- **When the auxiliary terms are harmless.** In the infinite-capacity (product-class) idealization behind Thm 3, an auxiliary loss on a *different output* does not change the Bayes target of the final output. These are:
  - deep supervision on z^t for t < T;
  - responsibility cross-entropy on `resp`;
  - existence BCE on a separate head;
  - the flow loss on a separate head.

  Each output is then its own Bayes estimator. The Hungarian assignment used by the responsibility CE is a discrete function of the prediction, so it contributes no gradient to z^T.
- **The only term that changes the target is NLL on the same output.** It is 0 by default.
- **So the pure phase is a finite-capacity heuristic, not a theorem condition.** Describe it that way, or drop it and remove a whole moving part (fade scheduling, the plateau trigger, two checkpoint pools) from the story.
- **Code check.** The gradient-equality test (`tests/test_new_features.py:87`) checks only `model.experts.nets[0][0].weight`, with `flow_head=False` and `nll=0`. If you keep the phase:
  - add an assertion in `QuotientLoss.forward` that raises when `aux_scale == 0` and (`nll > 0` or (`flow > 0` and not `flow_detach`));
  - check gradients over **all** backbone parameters.
- **Docs.** They disagree. `PROJECT_OVERVIEW.md` §3 says "the last 20% uses this loss alone"; the config says 10% fade plus 10% pure. Make them consistent.

### Note C: what R0-C-19 actually affects

- **Training.** In `gamma.py`, `log_sf_marginals` detaches the shape only in the *backward* pass. The forward responsibilities, and therefore what the network sees, are exact. With the quotient loss, a missing gradient path is an optimization inefficiency, not a change in the estimator's target.
- **The real bias is at inference.** `inference/refine.py:65` (`gradient_refine`) runs `nll.backward()` through `family.log_prob`. For censored and copula families, the shape then ignores the information in censored observations. The refined MLE will be biased, and so will anything else that uses torch-autograd likelihood gradients (the NLL ablation and self-consistency adaptation).
- **By contrast, `baselines/mle.py` is fine.** It uses scipy L-BFGS-B without an analytic Jacobian, i.e. finite differences, so it is unaffected.
- **Fix.** Write a custom `torch.autograd.Function` for `gammaincc`, with a central finite difference in the shape (step about 1e-5·a). This is accurate to about 1e-8 and has a few lines of code. A more elegant alternative uses the implicit-reparameterization identity ∂_a F(x; a) = −f(x; a) · ∂x/∂a (Figurnov, Mohamed & Mnih, NeurIPS 2018); PyTorch exposes ∂x/∂a for gamma sampling internally (check the current API before relying on it).

## 3. New issues the critique missed (R0-E-01 … R0-E-14)

### R0-E-01 [Major, theory] Canonicalization changes which estimator is learned

- **Where:** `models/base_net.py:46–56` (canonicalization by the per-marginal median); `simulation/simulator.py:145–155` (scale augmentation, ±4.6 in log scale, **one common factor per marginal shared by all components**); `losses/quotient.py:82–86` (loss in canonical coordinates).
- **Problem.** The network is exactly scale-equivariant and the loss is scale-invariant (log β − log c(D)). So it cannot represent the Bayes estimator for the proper, truncated scale prior. It converges to the **minimum-risk equivariant estimator**. As the augmentation range grows, that is the **generalized Bayes estimator under the right Haar prior on the scale group**. This is the Pitman (1939) estimator; see Eaton (1989) or Lehmann & Casella, ch. 3.
- **Consequence for Thm 3.** The correct statement is a Fréchet mean on Θ/(S_g × scale group), taken under a posterior with a flat prior on the common log-scale and the base prior on the *contrasts* log β_s − log β_1 (and on the shapes and weights).
- **Consequence for E3.** The NUTS or grid reference must use exactly this prior. Otherwise "matches the Bayes estimator" is tested against the wrong target, and you could get a spurious mismatch that looks like a failure of the method. This also partly answers R0-C-13: the canonicalized prior is analytic (flat × base) once stratification is off.
- **Why this is good news.** "Quotient-Pitman estimator: symmetry under both label permutation and scale" is a clean, correct and *new* statement that combines classical invariance theory with label switching. It is a better theoretical anchor than Thm 1 or Thm 4.

### R0-E-02 [Major, framing] Use "amortized Bayes vs MCMC" as the cost story, not "amortized vs EM"

- **The arithmetic.** R0-C-11 is right that you cannot beat EM on cost. The comparison that amortization wins is against **posterior inference**:
  - NUTS on a 2–3-component gamma mixture with n = 500 takes tens of seconds to minutes per dataset, mixes badly under overlap, and needs relabelling;
  - at 30 s per dataset, 10⁴ GHCN station-seasons cost about 80 CPU-hours;
  - an amortized estimator costs 3–14 GPU-hours to train once, then milliseconds per dataset.
- **So break-even against NUTS is around 10³ datasets.** That is *below* the size of your real data, the opposite of the situation against EM.
- **Rewrite H7 accordingly.** "AEM-Net matches the relabelled posterior mean (and, with the flow head, posterior credible sets) of NUTS within ε, at ≥ 100× lower total compute, on ≥ 10³ datasets." EM remains the reference for the *point MLE* and the hybrid.

### R0-E-03 [Major, design] Validate against the exact Bayes estimator using grid quadrature, not NUTS

- **The case.** For the E3 pilot, take g = 2, k = 1, and gamma components with **known shape**, unknown scales β₁, β₂ and π fixed at, say, 0.3. Alternatively, let π be unknown too, which makes the problem 3-D.
- **Why it is exact.** The posterior is 2-D (or 3-D), and can be computed **exactly** on a 200 × 200 grid (or a 100³ grid) in milliseconds per dataset for n ≤ 1000. From it you get, with no Monte-Carlo error and no label-switching heuristics:
  - the naive-MSE Bayes estimator (Thm 2a collapse);
  - the sorted Bayes estimator (Thm 2b bias);
  - the quotient Fréchet mean (Thm 3).
- **Why it matters.** It removes three problems at once:
  - NUTS mixing;
  - the choice of relabelling package;
  - the critic's objection that the reference is ill-defined (R0-C-13).
- **Procedure.** Train the four E3 loss variants on exactly this prior (no stratification; canonicalization either off, or on with the Haar prior of R0-E-01), and plot each network against its own exact target.
- **This is the single most important experiment in the project.** It is cheap enough to run this month on a CPU.

### R0-E-04 [Major, theory and experiments] The weakly identified (overlap) regime is where the paper lives

- **What is known.** Under strong overlap, finite mixtures are weakly identified.
  - The MLE's parameter rate degrades to n^(−1/4) or slower: Chen (1995); Ho & Nguyen (2016); Heinrich & Kahn (2018).
  - EM's contraction factor tends to 1 and its convergence becomes *sublinear*. In over-specified or near-singular mixtures, EM needs on the order of √n iterations to reach statistical precision: Dwivedi, Ho, Khamaru, Wainwright, Jordan & Yu (2020, *Ann. Statist.*).
- **What this means for the project:**
  - **(i) Thm 4.** Assumption A cannot hold uniformly over a prior that includes high overlap. The ⌈log₂ n⌉ schedule is wrong *by an order of magnitude in the exponent* there, not just by a constant (this strengthens R0-C-09).
  - **(ii) The hidden headline.** In this regime the Bayes estimator under a sensible prior beats the MLE's risk by a margin that *grows with n* over a range of n. NUTS is slow and multimodal there, and EM is slow. An amortized estimator that matches the exact Bayes estimator (R0-E-03) in this regime is a clean, defensible result. It also explains *why* E2's crossover sits where it does.
  - **(iii) Pre-register E2** with separation as a primary axis, and the expected n^(−1/4) vs n^(−1/2) slopes as reference lines.
- **Theory you could actually prove in time.** For g = 2, k = 1, one unknown scale each, give the quotient Bayes estimator's risk near the merge point, and compare it with the MLE's n^(−1/4) behaviour. That is a real Theorem; the current Thm 4 is not.

### R0-E-05 [Major, experiments] Add an explicit "basin-hit rate" metric

The defensible part of Thm 4 is empirical. Report the probability that EM started at the network's estimate converges to the **global** MLE, against k-means++ with 1, 10 and 100 restarts. Take the global MLE as the best of 100 restarts plus the network-initialized run. Stratify by overlap and n.

This metric:
- is cheap, because the batched EM already exists;
- is meaningful to statisticians;
- replaces Assumption B with something you can measure.

`baselines/batched_em.py` and the `x_em_steps` fields in the benchmark rows already give you most of it.

### R0-E-06 [Major, design] Rebuild E2's reference curve

- **Drop the CRB** (agreeing with R0-C-15).
- **Plot the Bayes risk of the exact quotient Bayes estimator** (grid for the pilot, relabelled NUTS where you scale up). It is the *actual* lower bound for your loss under your prior, so every estimator sits above it.
- **Optionally, add the van Trees inequality** (Gill & Levit 1995) as an analytic lower bound. It needs a prior density that is smooth and vanishes at the boundary, so the log-uniform box prior must be replaced by, for example, a cosine-squared bump on the box.

### R0-E-07 [Major, code] Compute the EM rate exactly instead of `theory/contraction.py`

- **The idea.** The EM map's Jacobian at the MLE is the matrix fraction of missing information, J = I_c⁻¹ I_m = I − I_c⁻¹ I_obs (Dempster, Laird & Rubin 1977; Meng & Rubin 1991).
  - For exponential-family components, the complete-data information I_c is in closed form.
  - The observed information I_obs follows from Louis (1982).
  - ρ(J) is its largest eigenvalue. J is similar to a symmetric matrix with eigenvalues in [0, 1), so this is well posed.
- **Implementation.** About 60 lines. It replaces the random-direction ratio (R0-C-10) with the exact local rate, and gives you the eigenvector, which is the slowest direction.
- **Use it to:**
  - set the K_n schedule per dataset (K = ⌈(1 + δ) log n / (2 log(1/ρ̂))⌉);
  - plot ρ̂ against overlap, which shows R0-E-04 directly.

### R0-E-08 [Major, architecture] Make Thm 1 literally true: an exact EM step plus a learned correction

- **For tractable families,** define the block as

  θ^t = EM(θ^{t−1}) ⊕ Δ_φ(slot state, pooled statistics),

  where ⊕ is addition in the u-coordinates and Δ_φ is zero-initialized.
- **What this gives you:**
  - (a) Thm 1 holds *for the actual model at initialization*, which answers R0-C-05 constructively;
  - (b) Thm 1(c) becomes non-vacuous at *finite* capacity, because the class contains T-step EM from the learned initialization;
  - (c) the ablation "Δ_φ ≡ 0" (pure unrolled EM from a learned initialization) becomes a strong, interpretable baseline that answers R0-C-01;
  - (d) the work connects to algorithm unrolling (Gregor & LeCun 2010; Monga, Li & Eldar 2021), which reviewers know.
- **For intractable families,** the current learned block stays. That is honest: there is no EM to embed.
- **Scale.** It is a modest code change. `ExactEMBlock` already computes EM(θ) from the shared E-block and pooling code.

## 5. Answers to D1–D5 (expert view)

- **D1 (lead with the method or the theory).** Lead with the **symmetry theory plus exact validation** (§4). The method alone is "Slot Attention + EM" (R0-C-02) and will be judged incremental. The theory alone is partly known (R0-C-04). The combination of a correct target (quotient-Pitman), an exact check, and a regime where it matters (weak identification) is what makes the paper. This is where I disagree with the critic's "lead with neither": lead with this combination, but only after R0-E-03 has run.
- **D2 (narrow Thm 3(iii) and Thm 4 now).** Yes.
  - Thm 3(iii): the general statement is fine *with* the measurable-selection and L² argument (note A). It does not need narrowing to g = 2.
  - Thm 4: narrow it to a one-step corollary (R0-E-14).
  - Put the narrowing effort into the merge-regime result (R0-E-04), for g = 2, k = 1.
- **D3 (is noisy gamma convincing).** No (I agree with R0-C-03). Any family whose density can be evaluated to 1e-6 will draw the same objection.
  - For the first paper, **drop the intractable claim**.
